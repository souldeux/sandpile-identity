The Sandpile Identity

Every stable pile of sand on a grid belongs to a group, and that group has an identity element. The identity is the pile that changes nothing when you add it. It turns out to be this, and why it looks this way is still an open question.

Check that it really is the identity

Build a random recurrent pile x on the same grid, add the identity e to it cell by cell, and let everything topple. If e is the identity, you get x back exactly, even though thousands of cells go unstable on the way.

The rule

Each cell of a grid holds some grains of sand. A cell with four or more grains is unstable. It topples: it loses four grains and gives one to each of its four neighbours. Grains pushed off the edge of the grid are gone for good, so every avalanche ends eventually.

The order in which you topple cells makes no difference to the final pile or to how many times each cell topples. Deepak Dhar proved this in 1990, and it is why the model is called abelian. It is also why the picture above can be computed with a simple stack of unstable cells in whatever order is convenient.

Adding piles

To add two stable piles, stack them cell by cell and topple until nothing is unstable. Some stable piles can never be reached again once the sand starts flowing. Leave those out, and the ones that remain, called recurrent, form a finite group under this addition.

The size of that group equals the number of spanning trees of the grid when its whole border is wired to one extra sink vertex. For an m×n grid it is the product of 4 − 2cos(jπ/(m+1)) − 2cos(kπ/(n+1)) over all j ≤ m, k ≤ n. The panel above shows it for the grid you picked. On a 2×2 grid there are 192 recurrent piles. On 256×256 the count has tens of thousands of digits.

Finding the identity

You might expect the identity to be the empty pile. It is not, because the empty pile is not recurrent. A recipe that works on any grid: fill every cell with 6 grains, stabilize, and subtract the result from 6. Then stabilize that difference.

The difference is equivalent to the empty pile, since subtracting a pile from its own stabilization only undoes topplings. It also has at least 3 grains everywhere, which is enough to guarantee the result is recurrent. The group has exactly one recurrent pile that is equivalent to nothing, and that pile is the identity. What is still unexplained

Look at the identity at 64, 128 and 256 cells. The same shape appears each time with finer detail: a solid square of 2s in the middle, cusped regions of 3s around it, and patches of periodic texture that meet along sharp lines. It looks like a picture converging to a limit.

In their 2017 survey, Levine and Peres list understanding the scaling limit of the sandpile identity as an open problem, solved in a special case by Caracciolo, Paoletti and Sportiello. On fractal graphs such as the Sierpiński gasket the question has been settled. Kaiser and Sava-Huss proved in 2023 that the identity averages out to a constant there. On the ordinary square grid, the picture above is still ahead of the proofs.

The same textures elsewhere

Switch to Single pile and drop tens of thousands of grains on one cell. The result is a rounded blob made from the same kinds of periodic patches. Pegden and Smart proved in 2013 that this pile has a scaling limit. Levine, Pegden and Smart then showed that the periodic patterns in that limit are organized by an Apollonian circle packing. Similar patches cover much of the identity, so it is tempting to think the two pictures have a common explanation. That link has not been proved.

Two things a longer look turned up

These come from my own computations with a C version of the same toppling code, not from the papers cited above. I have not found either one in the literature, though they may well be known.

Odd grids contain the even grid one size down

On a grid with an odd side, the identity has a cross of smaller values along its middle row and column, with a single empty cell at the centre. Delete that cross and push the four quarters together. What remains is exactly the identity of the even grid one size smaller, cell for cell. I checked every odd size from 5 to 257 and found no exceptions. You can check any size up to 129 here.

62 and 63

Here is how far I can explain it. Computing the even identity also tells you how many times each cell toppled on net. Build a pile on the odd grid by giving the four quarters those same counts and letting each cross cell copy its neighbour's count. That pile differs from the empty pile only by topplings, and in the quarters it holds exactly the even identity. On the cross it holds the second difference of the counts along the row beside the midline, and at the centre it holds 0. If those cross values stay between 1 and 3, the pile is stable and also recurrent. The recurrence argument folds the grid along its mirror lines, as Florescu, Morar, Perkinson, Salter and Xu do for symmetric sandpiles. A stable, recurrent pile that differs from the empty pile only by topplings has to be the identity.

The missing piece is that bound on the toppling counts. For every odd size from 5 to 129 I computed them directly: they are always 1 or 2, and they match the real identity's cross exactly. Proving the bound would need control of the toppling counts, which is the same kind of problem that keeps the scaling limit open.

Rectangles show that the bound is doing real work. Take a rectangle with an odd width, delete its middle column, and compare with the rectangle one column narrower. The two identities match on every height up to about 1.4 times the width and differ on every height beyond it. Where they differ, the construction above is what breaks. The values it puts on the middle column drop to 0 in two places with only 1s between them, and that stretch stops its pile from being recurrent. Checking for that pattern predicted the outcome correctly for all 900 rectangles I tested. So symmetry alone cannot prove the square case. A proof has to show that squares stay on the safe side of this threshold.

Le Borgne and Rossin’s method gives a sharper way to state what is missing. They start from an explicit pile that differs from the empty pile only by topplings: 2 grains on every cell, plus tall stacks along the four corner diagonals. Stabilizing it produces the identity. Rebuilt here, their avalanche reproduces the identity exactly, and the missing bound turns into a statement about how often its cells topple. On the row beside the midline those counts would need to be concave, with the slope never dropping by more than 1 from one cell to the next. On a 32-wide grid the counts along that row are 8, 15, 22, …, 71, then 77, 82, 86, 89, 91, 92. The slope stays at 7 and then drops by exactly 1 at each step. That pattern holds on every even size from 4 to 128, and it would be enough to finish the proof.

I could not prove it. Their own arguments show that toppling counts decrease along lines, which is a first-order fact. Concavity is second-order, and rounding down to whole topplings can break it, so their step-by-step induction does not carry over. The pattern also appears on this one row only, where the avalanches from the top-left and bottom-left corners meet head-on. Every other row is irregular, so there is no grid-wide property to build an induction on. Closing the gap would take an exact description of that collision.

The square takes up 5/12 of the width

On even grids the central square of 2s grows in proportion to the width. Across every even width from 8 to 256, plus 512 and 1024, its side is close to 5n/12 + 0.6. The ratio wobbles because the side only grows in steps of 2, but it closes in on 5/12 as the grid grows.

The obvious rival guess is √2 − 1 ≈ 0.4142. Compare how far each slope misses the measured sides. With 5/12 the average miss stays flat, +0.60 cells on widths 16 to 128 and +0.62 cells on widths 130 to 512. With √2 − 1 it grows from +0.77 to +1.10 as the grid gets bigger, so that line drifts away from the data. At 1024 the measured side is 428. The 5/12 line predicts about 427, and sides are always even. The √2 − 1 line would miss by almost 4 cells. This is a numerical fit, not a proof. Some sizes also carry one-off details: at 512 a faint octagon of 2s surrounds the square, and it is gone again at 1024.
